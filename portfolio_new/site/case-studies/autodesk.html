This was an industry project with Autodesk. In line with the University's dissemination guidance, materials supplied by Autodesk, the findings from stakeholder and customer interviews, and product-specific detail aren't shared here. This page covers the problem, the methods, my role and the outcome, using generalised content only.

Two workflows. One platform. No way to connect them.

An industry project with Autodesk, run as a 6-week Agile sprint within the MSc UX industry module, in a team of four. The problem space was new to us: large-scale construction coordination with BIM software. We worked from a live brief to define scope, then designed a workflow connecting two analyses that users need together but the product keeps apart.

Autodesk ACC Industry project · MSc UX · University of Lancashire 6-week sprint · Agile Scrum Heuristic eval · Thematic analysis · Prototyping 7 min read

My role: team lead (team of 4) · thematic analysis · IA

the problem

Two forms of analysis. Completely separate places.

Autodesk Construction Cloud has Change Analysis and Clash Analysis, both critical to BIM workflows. In the application, they are hosted in entirely different parts of the platform. In users' real work, they are closely related.

change analysis
What was added, removed, or modified

Tracks modifications made to 3D building model objects across versions. Essential for understanding what moved, who moved it, and when.

clash analysis
Where objects overlap in space

Identifies when model objects physically overlap, conflicts that must be resolved before a building can be constructed. Users want to trace a clash back to the change that introduced it.

When a clash is found, users want to trace it back to the change that introduced it. To resolve it, they need to change one or both objects, and the brief asked whether those changes could be suggested, or recorded against the clash as history. In the product, the two analyses live in separate places.

research & discovery

Large-scale analysis. Two themes rose above everything else.

Three phases across the 6-week sprint: Discovery, Ideation, Evaluation. Autodesk supplied background data, insights and personas (not shared here). We added public data, a heuristic evaluation, benchmarking, and interviews with stakeholders and customers.

01Heuristic Evaluation + Large-Scale Content Analysis

We ran a heuristic evaluation of the platform's workflows (Nielsen's 10 heuristics). Alongside it, we collected over 25,000 public comments about ACC, Clash and Change Analysis, model coordination, design collaboration and Navisworks, from Autodesk forums, social media, YouTube and Reddit.

  • • AI helped us decide which comments were worth coding. We then filtered manually in Excel, ending with 160 actionable insights. Codes and themes were defined by the team, using thematic and sentiment analysis.
  • • Key finding from the heuristic evaluation: an inconsistent UI and limited feedback make it hard to connect model changes with clashes, track them over time, and move between workflows. It was also unclear who made a change, where it came from, and who should resolve the issue.
02Thematic Analysis — Five Themes, Two Dominant

Codes were grouped into five overarching themes. Two became the focus: Workflow Challenges and Visibility Gaps.

03Benchmark Analysis + Analogous Inspiration

We benchmarked platforms directly and indirectly related to the problem space to find best practice and limitations. One example stood out.

  • • Navisworks tracks clashes through a clear status lifecycle: New → Active → Resolved
  • • This gives teams a shared view of what has been dealt with and what still needs attention
  • • Analogous Inspiration surfaced useful patterns from outside the BIM field

The research pointed to a fragmented coordination ecosystem: no data continuity between detecting a clash and tracking the issue, and high cognitive load from moving across multiple tools.

from research to direction

Workflow Challenges and Visibility Gaps converged on one core problem.

HMW questions generated from thematic analysis, prioritised using an impact × frequency matrix, then formalised into a MoSCoW requirements set.

problem statement

Users face a critical visibility gap: there is no functional bridge between "what changed" and "what broke". Teams can't establish what caused a new issue, so they reconcile manually, which raises the risk of oversight, weakens collaborative reviews and obscures the audit trail behind decisions.

key hmw directions
Connect clash to change
HMW trace a detected clash back to the specific change that introduced it, in a single workflow rather than across two separate tools?
Reduce cognitive overhead
HMW reduce the effort of navigating large volumes of clash data without sufficient contextual control?
Proactive over reactive
HMW move teams from a reactive stance, discovering clashes after they occur, to a proactive one?
Create an audit trail
HMW record how clashes were resolved and surface that history when similar conflicts arise in future model iterations?
wireframes and ideation
solution

Two contributions. One unified principle.

We developed the prototype through iterative wireframing in the Ideation and Evaluation phases, using the problem statement, the personas and the interview insights.

01Object / Filter-Based Approach to Clash & Change Analysis

One of the core friction points identified during research was cognitive overhead: navigating large volumes of clash data without sufficient contextual control. Users were forced to manually sift through dense lists.

The prototype introduced an object and filter-based interaction model, letting users isolate, group and prioritise clashes by parameters such as discipline, severity or model element type. The aim was to give users more control over what they review and cut the noise in clash review sessions. That hasn't been tested with end users.

02Predictive Clash Forecasting

The second contribution addressed a more forward-looking need: moving teams from reactive to proactive clash management.

Rather than only surfacing clashes after they occur, the prototype explored a predictive forecasting model that could signal likely conflict zones from patterns in model changes over time. It was informed by research showing that late discovery of clashes causes delays and rework. It's an exploration, not a validated model.

The aim across both contributions: let users see which change sits behind a clash, in one workflow rather than two tools, and reduce the decision fatigue of reviewing them.

evaluation

The research points one way. The prototype is untested with end users.

Three limitations, each with a concrete next step.

✓
Problem direction — Workflow Challenges and Visibility Gaps were the two key themes across 25,000+ public comments from several sources.
✓
Benchmark pattern — Navisworks' Clash Status Lifecycle (New → Active → Resolved) is an existing model, so the direction has precedent.
✓
Expert interviews — used to cross-reference the public data (findings not shared here).
!
End-user testing — Not conducted. The prototype was not tested with real BIM Managers or Engineers. Required before any deployment decision.
01Data collection scope
  • GapPublic datasets skew toward frustrated users. Power users, satisfied users, and those who simply get on with the tool are largely invisible. Findings are directionally valid but need validation against a broader sample.
  • Next StepStructured research with a representative cross-section of ACC users across disciplines and project types.
02No end-user testing
  • GapThe prototype was not tested with real BIM Managers or Engineers. Instead it was tested with a developer who worked on the project before, to get formal, technical feedback on our ideas. Given the domain complexity (high-stakes construction workflows), this is a significant gap that stakeholder interviews cannot substitute for.
  • Next StepTask-based usability study with BIM Managers and Engineers using the integrated prototype in a real project context.
03Generalisability of platform findings
  • GapFindings from the platform audit may not generalise beyond the specific workflows studied. ACC serves a wide range of construction disciplines with different coordination priorities.
  • Next StepValidation of the predictive forecasting model against historical clash and change data from live ACC projects across multiple disciplines.
reflection

What worked, what didn't.

What Worked
✓Filtering at scale. Reading 25,000+ comments line by line wasn't feasible in six weeks. We used AI to help pick what to code, then filtered manually in Excel down to 160 insights before coding. The trade-off: that first filter shaped what we saw.
✓Pushing past the brief. The challenge asked how to integrate two existing views. Predictive forecasting was our attempt at a different mental model, proactive rather than reactive. The research showed late discovery hurts; it didn't show prediction is possible, which is why it stays an exploration.
✓Benchmark as a design anchor. Navisworks already had a clash status lifecycle, which gave us an existing pattern to learn from instead of designing resolution tracking from scratch.
What Didn't / What's Unresolved
✕No end-user testing, and public data as a proxy. Both are covered under Evaluation above. For a domain this technically specific, the missing end-user testing matters most.
?Role-based view logic unresolved. BIM Managers, Engineers, and Architects have different needs when reviewing clashes and changes. The prototype addressed integration but the permission model and role-based view logic would require a further sprint to resolve properly.

The brief asked for two levels of work: workflow design (when and where to show clash and change analysis, and to which users) and UI design (how the two sets of results interact in the interface). We worked on both.
